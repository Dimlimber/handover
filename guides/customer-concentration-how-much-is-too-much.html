Customer concentration: how much is too much when you sell?

One big customer can lower your price or sink your sale. Here is where buyers and lenders start to worry, and what to do if you can't change the numbers in time.

By Dimitri, Handover · 20 September 2026

The guide

The short answer

Buyers and lenders start to worry when one customer makes up more than about 20% of revenue, or the top five make up more than half. It doesn't stop a sale, but it usually lowers the price or changes the terms. You can reduce the concern with contracts, a long track record and relationships that go beyond the owner.

Why buyers care

A buyer is paying today for income they expect tomorrow. If a third of that income depends on one customer's goodwill, the buyer is taking a bet on a relationship they do not have yet. And that relationship is usually with you.

Lenders feel the same. Those financing a purchase will often ask about any customer above roughly 20% of revenue, and what would happen if that customer left.

Where the lines are

These are rules of thumb, not rules. They vary by industry and by buyer.

Largest customer's share of revenue How buyers tend to react
Under 10% No concern
10% to 20% They will ask about it
20% to 35% A real issue. Expect a lower price or protective terms
Over 35% Many buyers and lenders walk away, unless there is a long contract

Also look at your top five customers together. Over half of revenue from five customers raises the same questions.

How it changes a deal

Buyers rarely say no outright. They protect themselves instead:

  • A lower price. The risk comes off the multiple.
  • An earn-out. Part of the price is paid later, only if the customer stays.
  • A longer handover. You stay for a year instead of three months, to transfer the relationship.
  • Seller financing. You lend the buyer part of the price, so you share the risk.

None of these is necessarily bad. But you want to choose them, not have them forced on you.

Work out your own numbers first

Export three years of sales by customer and calculate:

  1. Your largest customer's share of revenue, each year
  2. Your top five customers' share, each year
  3. How many years each of your top ten has been buying from you
  4. What share of last year's revenue came from customers who also bought the year before

Owners are often surprised, in both directions. Know your numbers before a buyer works them out for you.

If you have a year or more

  • Grow the rest. You don't need to shrink the big customer. Growing other accounts brings the percentage down.
  • Get a contract. A multi-year agreement with your largest customer changes the conversation completely. Check that it survives a change of ownership.
  • Widen the relationship. Make sure several people at your business know several people at theirs.

If you are selling soon

You can't change the numbers, so change how risky they look:

  • Show the history. A customer who has bought every month for fourteen years is not the same risk as one who arrived last spring. Put the tenure in front of the buyer.
  • Show why they stay. Are you the only approved supplier? Would switching be costly or slow for them? Say so, specifically.
  • Show it isn't personal. If your operations manager already handles the account day to day, that matters more than any argument.
  • Offer the introduction. Plan how and when the buyer meets the customer, with your endorsement. Usually late in the process, under confidentiality.
  • Be open about it early. Concentration disclosed in the first conversation is a fact to be priced. Concentration discovered in due diligence is a reason to walk away.

The other kinds of concentration

Buyers ask the same questions about a single supplier you depend on, a single salesperson who brings in most of the work, or a single referral source. The same answers apply: contracts, history, and relationships that belong to the business and not to one person.
