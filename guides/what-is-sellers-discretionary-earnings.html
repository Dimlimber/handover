What is seller's discretionary earnings (SDE), and how do you calculate yours?

SDE is the number most small businesses are valued on. Here is what it means, how to work it out from your tax return, and the mistakes that make buyers distrust it.

By Dimitri, Handover · 20 September 2026

The guide

The short answer

Seller's discretionary earnings (SDE) is the total financial benefit one full-time owner takes from a business in a year. You calculate it by starting with pre-tax profit and adding back the owner's pay, interest, depreciation, and any personal or one-off expenses. Small businesses are usually priced as a multiple of SDE.

Why your tax return understates your business

A good accountant keeps your taxable profit low. That is their job. Your pay, the truck, the phone, the family health plan and the trip that was partly a conference all reduce profit, legally.

A buyer is asking a different question: if I owned this, how much money would it produce for me? SDE answers it.

The calculation

Start with Pre-tax profit from the tax return or profit-and-loss statement
Add back The owner's salary, payroll taxes and benefits
Add back Interest, depreciation and amortisation
Add back Personal expenses paid by the business
Add back One-off costs that will not recur
Take off One-off income that will not recur
Equals Seller's discretionary earnings

A worked example

A plumbing company reports $212,000 of profit on its tax return. The owner pays herself $150,000.
Profit on the tax return $212,000
Owner's salary and payroll taxes + $162,000
Depreciation and interest + $41,000
Family vehicle, phones and travel + $31,000
One-off legal bill for a lease dispute + $18,000
SDE $464,000

The business she thought made a couple of hundred thousand dollars produces more than twice that for its owner. The difference changes the price.

SDE or EBITDA?

Buyers of smaller businesses, roughly those earning under $1 million, think in SDE because they expect to work in the business and take the owner's pay themselves.

Buyers of larger businesses use EBITDA (earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation). EBITDA assumes a manager is paid a market wage to run the business, so only the owner's pay above that wage is added back. If a manager would cost $110,000 and you pay yourself $150,000, the add-back is $40,000, not $150,000.

Use the wrong one and your price will look wrong to every buyer who sees it.

Four mistakes that make buyers distrust your SDE

  1. Add-backs with no paperwork. A buyer will only accept an add-back they can trace to an invoice or a statement line. See which add-backs buyers accept.
  2. Adding back costs the business needs. Your spouse's salary is only an add-back if your spouse does not really work there, or is paid above the going rate.
  3. Counting two owners as one. SDE assumes one full-time owner. If two of you work in the business, a buyer will subtract a market wage for the second.
  4. One good year. Buyers look at three years and weight the trend. A single strong year does not set the price.

What to do with the number

Work out SDE for each of the last three years. If it is rising, that is your best sales argument. If it is falling or uneven, you need the explanation ready before a buyer asks.
