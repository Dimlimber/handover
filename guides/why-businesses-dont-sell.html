Why small businesses don't sell, and what to fix first

Most failed sales come down to the same five problems. Here is how each one looks to a buyer, and the order to fix them in.

By Dimitri, Handover · 20 September 2026

The guide

The short answer

Small businesses fail to sell for five main reasons: earnings the buyer can't verify, a business that depends on its owner, a few customers making up most of the revenue, paperwork that won't transfer, and a price with no evidence behind it. All five can be fixed before listing, and the financial records should come first.

A note on the statistics

You will often read that 70% or 80% of listed businesses never sell. We looked for the source of that figure and could not find one, so we don't repeat it.

What is well documented is that most sellers arrive unprepared. In the IBBA and M&A Source Market Pulse survey for the second quarter of 2026, brokers reported that 60% to 90% of sellers had done less than a year of planning, or none. And sales are slow: BizBuySell's Insight Report put the median time on the market at around 170 days for businesses sold in 2025. Those are the ones that sold.

The five reasons

1. The buyer can't verify the earnings

The owner says the business makes $400,000. The tax return says $180,000. The difference is real, but it is scattered across three years of card statements and nobody has written it down.

A buyer might take your word. Their bank won't. Many small purchases are financed with SBA-backed loans, and lenders want three years of tax returns and statements that tie together. No proof, no loan, no sale.

Fix: recast three years of financials, with every adjustment documented. See SDE and add-backs.

2. The business is the owner

The customers call your mobile. You price every job. The one supplier who matters is a friend of thirty years. A buyer looks at that and sees a job they would have to learn from you, with customers who may leave when you do.

Fix: this takes longest, so start early. See how to make your business run without you.

3. Too much revenue from too few customers

One customer at 35% of revenue is a risk a buyer has to price. Often they price it by offering less, or by holding back part of the payment until they see whether the customer stays.

Fix: you often can't change the numbers quickly, but you can change how risky they look. See customer concentration.

4. The paperwork won't transfer

A lease with eight months left and no right to assign it. A licence in the owner's personal name. A key contract that ends on a change of ownership. These surface late, when the buyer's lawyer reads the documents, and they kill deals that were otherwise agreed.

Fix: read your own documents before a buyer does. See the documents buyers and lenders ask for.

5. The price has nothing behind it

"I need $2 million to retire" is a fact about the owner, not the business. A price the earnings can't support gets no serious enquiries. The listing goes stale, and buyers start to wonder what is wrong with it.

Fix: base the price on recast earnings and on what similar businesses have actually sold for. See what is my small business worth?

What to fix first

Order Fix Why this order
1 Financial records Everything else, including the price, depends on knowing the real earnings
2 Owner dependence It takes the longest, so it needs the earliest start
3 Paperwork Cheap to check, and the problems take time to solve (landlords are slow)
4 Customers Mostly a matter of analysis and contracts
5 Story and price Last, because it rests on the other four

If your business is already listed and not selling

The same five reasons apply. Ask your broker what buyers have said when they walked away. It will almost always be one of these. None of them requires taking the business off the market to fix, and your broker will thank you for fixing it.
