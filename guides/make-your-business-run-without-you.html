How to make your business run without you

A business that depends on its owner sells for less, or not at all. Here is how buyers test for it, and a practical twelve-month plan to fix it.

By Dimitri, Handover · 20 September 2026

The guide

The short answer

To make a business less dependent on its owner, move three things to your team: customer relationships, day-to-day decisions, and the knowledge of how things are done. Start by taking a two-week break and writing down everything that waited for you. Most owners need six to twelve months to make a visible difference.

Why it matters more than anything else

A buyer is buying what the business will earn after you have gone. If the customers, the pricing, the supplier deals and the know-how all live with you, then what they are really buying is a job, plus the hope that you will teach it to them before you leave.

They will pay less for that. Some will not pay at all. And the bank lending them the money will ask the same question.

How buyers test for it

They rarely ask "does the business depend on you?" They ask:

  • "What does a typical week look like for you?"
  • "When did you last take two weeks off? What happened?"
  • "If your biggest customer has a problem, who do they call?"
  • "Who does the quotes?"
  • "Who would run this if you were ill for three months?"
  • "How long would you stay on after the sale?"

The last one is a test too. An owner who says "as long as it takes" is telling the buyer the business needs them.

The two-week test

Take a real break. Two weeks, phone off apart from emergencies. When you come back, write down:

  1. Every decision that waited for you
  2. Every customer who asked for you by name
  3. Everything that went wrong, and why
  4. Everything nobody else knew how to do

That list is your plan. It is more accurate than anything you could write from your desk.

A twelve-month plan

Months Do this
1 Take the two-week test. Write the list.
1–2 Choose your second-in-command. If nobody fits, start hiring. It may be the best investment you make before selling.
2–4 Write down the ten tasks only you can do. One page each: a checklist, not a manual.
2–6 Introduce your second-in-command to your ten largest customers and your key suppliers. Have them lead the next routine conversation with each.
3–6 Hand over one whole area: quoting, scheduling, purchasing or hiring. Let them make the decisions, including a few you would have made differently.
6–9 Hand over a second area. Move yourself to a weekly review, not a daily one.
9–12 Take a month off. Fix what broke.

Keeping the person you now depend on

Once a manager is running things, the buyer's worry moves from you to them. Answer it in advance:

  • A stay bonus that pays out six or twelve months after a sale
  • A clear role and title, in writing
  • An honest conversation at the right moment. Most brokers advise telling key staff late, and with a plan for them. Take advice on timing.

What it looks like to a buyer when it is done

  • You can describe your week, and it is mostly oversight
  • Customers name other people at the business when asked who they deal with
  • There is a folder of written procedures, and staff use it
  • You took a month off, and revenue did not move
  • You can honestly offer a short handover, because a short one is all that is needed

That business sells faster, to more buyers, for more money. It is also a better business to own in the meantime, which is worth remembering if the sale takes longer than you hoped.

If you don't have twelve months

Do the two-week test anyway, write the procedures, and make the introductions. Then be straightforward with buyers: offer a longer handover, and price the business for what it is. A buyer can work with an owner-dependent business. They cannot work with a surprise.
