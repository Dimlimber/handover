Add-backs: what buyers will and won't accept

Add-backs can raise the price of a small business by hundreds of thousands of dollars, but only if a buyer believes them. Here is which ones hold up, which don't, and how to document them.

By Dimitri, Handover · 20 September 2026

The guide

The short answer

An add-back is an expense in your books that a new owner would not have to pay, such as your own salary, personal costs run through the business, or a one-off legal bill. Buyers accept add-backs that are documented, clearly personal or clearly one-off. They reject ones that are vague, recurring or needed to run the business.

Why add-backs matter so much

Small businesses sell for a multiple of what they earn. If businesses like yours sell for around two and a half times earnings, every $10,000 of accepted add-backs is worth about $25,000 on the price. Every $10,000 a buyer throws out costs you the same.

So buyers and their accountants go through add-backs line by line. It is usually the most argued-over part of a sale.

Add-backs buyers usually accept

Add-back What makes it hold up
Owner's salary, payroll taxes and benefits Payroll records. For larger businesses, only the amount above a manager's market wage
Owner's health, life and disability insurance The policy and the payments
Personal vehicle, fuel and insurance It must be a vehicle the business does not need
Personal travel, meals and entertainment Card statements with the personal items marked
Family members on payroll who don't work in the business Payroll records, and honesty about who does what
One-off professional fees A lawsuit, a one-time consulting project, the cost of preparing for this sale
One-off repairs or losses A flood, a break-in, an equipment failure that won't repeat
Depreciation, amortisation and interest Straight from the tax return
Rent above market, if you own the building A market rent estimate from a commercial agent

Add-backs buyers usually reject

  • "Discretionary" marketing, training or software. If the business grew while spending it, a buyer assumes the spending is needed.
  • A family member's pay when they really do the job. The buyer will have to pay someone.
  • Repairs and maintenance that happen every year. Recurring is not one-off, whatever it was called.
  • Cash sales that were never recorded. You cannot ask a buyer to pay for income you told the tax authority you did not have. Do not raise it.
  • Anything without paper. "About $20,000 of my personal stuff goes through the card" is worth nothing. A list of 140 transactions with dates and amounts is worth $20,000.

How to document them

  1. Export three years of transactions from your accounting software and your business cards.
  2. Mark every personal, family and one-off item. Be strict: if you would hesitate to explain it to a stranger, leave it out.
  3. For each one, keep the invoice or statement line. One folder per year.
  4. Build a one-page schedule: the item, the year, the amount, the reason, the document.
  5. Total it by year, and add it to profit to get your seller's discretionary earnings.

A buyer's accountant should be able to pick any line on the schedule and find the proof in under a minute.

The cleaner way

Add-backs are an argument you have to win. Expenses that were never in the books need no argument at all.

If a sale is a year or more away, stop running personal costs through the business now. Your tax bill goes up a little. Your reported profit goes up by the same amount, and a buyer will accept every dollar of it. At a multiple of two or three, that is a good trade.

How many is too many?

There is no rule, but when add-backs are more than about a third of claimed earnings, buyers start to doubt the whole set, and lenders get uneasy. Fewer, larger, well-documented add-backs are more persuasive than a long list of small ones.
