Selling an HVAC, plumbing or electrical business: what buyers look for

Home-services businesses are in demand, and buyers are paying well for the right ones. Here is what separates a business that attracts several offers from one that sits.

By Dimitri, Handover · 20 September 2026

The guide

The short answer

Buyers of HVAC, plumbing and electrical businesses pay most for recurring service-agreement revenue, a team of licensed technicians who will stay, a business that doesn't depend on the owner's license or relationships, and clean financials. Private-equity-backed groups are actively buying, but they are selective, and preparation decides who gets the premium.

Why buyers want these businesses

People need heating, cooling, water and power whatever the economy is doing. The work can't be sent offshore or done over the internet. And the industry is still made up mostly of independent, owner-run firms. Capstone Partners put US HVAC contracting alone at about $159 billion in revenue in 2026, most of it independents.

That combination has drawn in private-equity-backed groups, which buy local firms and combine them. They now sit alongside the traditional buyers: a competitor across town, a senior technician with a bank loan, or an individual leaving a corporate career.

More buyers is good news for sellers. But the groups in particular have seen hundreds of businesses, and they know exactly what they are looking for.

What they look for

1. Service agreements and repeat revenue

Maintenance plans are the most valuable thing you can show. They are predictable, they lead to replacement work, and they belong to the business and not to you. Know your numbers: how many agreements, what they bring in, and how many renew each year.

Buyers also look at the mix. Service and replacement work for existing customers is worth more than new-construction work won by bidding, which is lumpier, lower-margin and tied to a few builders.

2. Technicians who will stay

Skilled, licensed people are the hardest thing to find in the trades. A buyer wants to know how long your team has been with you, how they are paid, and whether they will stay. High turnover, or a team of relatives who will leave with you, lowers the price.

3. A business that isn't you

Whose license does the business operate under? If it is yours alone, that is a problem to solve before you sell: a qualifying license-holder who is staying needs to be in place. Rules differ by state, so check with your licensing board.

Beyond the license, the usual tests apply. Who quotes the big jobs? Who do the commercial accounts call? Who dispatches?

4. Clean books, and job-level numbers

Three years of financials that tie to the tax returns, with documented add-backs. Trades businesses often have more than most: trucks, fuel, tools, phones and family on the payroll.

Better buyers will also ask for gross margin by type of work (service, replacement, new construction), average ticket, and revenue per technician. If your software can produce them, have them ready.

5. Customers and data

A customer list in a proper field-service system, with equipment age and service history, is an asset a buyer can market to. A box of paper invoices isn't. Concentration matters here too: one builder or property manager providing a third of revenue will worry a buyer.

6. Fleet, equipment and premises

A list of vehicles with age, mileage and condition. Whether they are owned or financed. Whether the lease on the shop can be assigned, or whether you own it and will lease it to the buyer.

What these businesses sell for

Smaller firms sell for a multiple of seller's discretionary earnings, typically in the range of two to three times, in line with small businesses generally. Larger, well-run firms with strong recurring revenue and a management team can attract materially higher multiples of EBITDA from the groups. The gap between the two is the reward for preparation and size.

Be wary of headline multiples quoted in trade press. They usually describe much larger businesses than the typical independent.

Who to sell to

Buyer What to expect
A private-equity-backed group Often the highest price for the right business. Usually wants you to stay one to three years, and may ask you to keep a share. Thorough due diligence.
A local competitor Knows the business. May only want the customers and the technicians. Confidentiality matters most here.
A key employee Continuity for staff and customers. Usually needs an SBA loan and some seller financing.
An individual buyer Will depend heavily on your handover and your team. Almost always SBA-financed, so clean books are essential.

Six things to do in the next six months

  1. Count your service agreements and work out the renewal rate.
  2. Sort out the license question.
  3. Recast three years of financials and document every add-back.
  4. Put your customer and equipment records into one system.
  5. Hand dispatch, quoting or a major account to someone else.
  6. Talk to a broker who sells trades businesses, so you know what buyers in your area are paying for.
