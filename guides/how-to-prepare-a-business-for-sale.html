How to prepare a business for sale: a checklist

The five things buyers and their lenders check before they pay for a small business, and what to do about each one before you list.

By Dimitri, Handover · 20 September 2026

The guide

The short answer

Preparing a business for sale means fixing five things before a buyer looks: financial records a buyer can verify, a business that runs without you, a customer base that isn't concentrated, paperwork that transfers cleanly, and a price with evidence behind it. Most of it can be done in three to twelve months.

Why preparation matters

Most owners sell a business once. Most buyers, and every lender, have looked at dozens. They know where small businesses hide their problems, and they check the same places every time.

Owners rarely prepare. In the IBBA and M&A Source Market Pulse survey for the second quarter of 2026, business brokers reported that between 60% and 90% of sellers, depending on the size of the business, had done less than a year of exit planning or none at all. That is an opening. A prepared business stands out.

The checklist

1. Financial records a buyer can verify

  • Three years of profit-and-loss statements, balance sheets and business tax returns, in one folder
  • Statements that agree with the tax returns, or a written explanation where they don't
  • A list of every personal, family and one-off expense in the books, with the document that proves each one (see add-backs)
  • A calculation of what the business really earns for its owner (see seller's discretionary earnings)
  • No new personal expenses through the business from today

2. A business that runs without you

  • A named person who can run things day to day
  • Your ten largest customers know someone at the business other than you
  • The tasks only you can do, written down
  • A real two-week absence, and a list of what went wrong

More on this in how to make your business run without you.

3. Customers a buyer can count on

  • Sales by customer for three years, in one spreadsheet
  • The share of revenue from your largest customer, and your largest five
  • The share of last year's revenue that came from customers who also bought the year before
  • Contracts or service agreements for your biggest and most regular customers

See customer concentration.

4. Operations and paperwork that transfer

  • A lease that can be assigned to a new owner, with years left to run
  • Licences, permits and insurance current, and transferable
  • Key supplier and customer contracts checked for change-of-ownership clauses
  • An equipment list with age and condition
  • Employee roles, pay and any agreements, documented

The full list is in the documents buyers and lenders ask for.

5. A story and a price with evidence

  • One page on what the business does, why customers choose it and why it earns what it earns
  • Three realistic ways a new owner could grow it
  • A price based on recast earnings and on what similar businesses have actually sold for (see what is my business worth)

How long it takes

What Typical time
Gathering and organising financial records 2 to 6 weeks
Documenting add-backs and recasting three years 3 to 6 weeks
Customer analysis 1 to 2 weeks
Checking the lease, licences and contracts 2 to 4 weeks, longer if the landlord is slow
Reducing dependence on the owner 3 to 12 months
Twelve clean months with no personal expenses in the books 12 months

The paperwork is quick. Changing how the business runs is slow. That is the reason to start early, even if a sale is a couple of years away.

What to do first

Start with the numbers, because everything else depends on them. Until you know what the business really earns, you cannot know what it is worth, which fixes are worth making, or whether now is the time.
