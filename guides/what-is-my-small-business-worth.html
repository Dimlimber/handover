What is my small business worth?

How small businesses are actually priced, what the multiples are, and what moves you to the top or bottom of the range. With a worked example.

By Dimitri, Handover · 20 September 2026

The guide

The short answer

Most small businesses sell for a multiple of what they earn for their owner. Businesses worth under about $2 million typically sell for around two to three times seller's discretionary earnings, and larger ones for around four times EBITDA or more. Where you land in the range depends on how verifiable, transferable and dependable those earnings are.

How small businesses are priced

Not on revenue, not on what you have put in, and not on what you need to retire. A buyer pays for the income the business will produce for them, adjusted for how confident they are that it will continue.

So the price is two numbers multiplied together:

Earnings × a multiple

You have more control over both than you might think.

The earnings

For smaller businesses the measure is seller's discretionary earnings (SDE): what one full-time owner takes out in a year, including their pay and perks. For larger ones it is EBITDA, which assumes a paid manager.

Most owners underestimate their real earnings, because their books are kept to keep tax down. Recasting the books, with documented add-backs, often reveals significantly more.

The multiple

Multiples rise with size, because larger businesses are less risky and attract more buyers. Recent IBBA and M&A Source Market Pulse surveys of business brokers have reported medians of roughly:

Sale price Typical multiple
Under $500,000 About 2 × SDE
$500,000 to $1 million About 2.8 × SDE
$1 million to $2 million About 3 × SDE
$2 million to $5 million About 4 × EBITDA
$5 million and above Higher, and more varied

BizBuySell's Insight Report showed the average small business selling for about 2.7 times cash flow in mid-2026. These are medians across all industries. Yours will differ: ask a broker who sells businesses like yours what they are seeing.

A worked example

A distribution business with recast SDE of $400,000, in a range of 2.5 to 3.2 times:

Multiple Price
Bottom of the range 2.5 × $1,000,000
Top of the range 3.2 × $1,280,000

Same business, same earnings, a $280,000 difference. What decides it?

What moves you up or down the range

Towards the top Towards the bottom
Three years of clean, reconciled financials Books that don't match the tax returns
Earnings rising, or steady with a clear explanation Falling or erratic earnings
A manager who runs things day to day Everything goes through the owner
No customer over 10–15% of revenue One customer over 25%
Repeat or contracted revenue Every sale won from scratch
A long, transferable lease A short lease, or a landlord who won't engage
Written procedures, trained staff Know-how in people's heads
A believable plan for growth "There's lots of potential"

Notice that almost everything in the left-hand column can be worked on before you sell.

Things that are not the price

  • Stock is often added on top, at cost.
  • Property is valued and sold, or leased, separately.
  • Cash and debts usually stay with you. Most small sales are asset sales: the buyer gets the business, you keep the bank account and pay off the loans.
  • Deal terms matter as much as the headline. $1.2 million, all cash at closing, is worth more than $1.3 million with a third paid over five years if things go well.

Rules of thumb, and why to be careful

You will hear that a business in your industry sells for "one times revenue" or "so much per customer". These are averages of deals that were really priced on earnings. They are a sanity check at best.

How to get a real number

  1. Recast your last three years to find your true earnings.
  2. Ask a broker who sells in your industry and price range for an opinion of value. Many will give one free, hoping for the listing.
  3. If you need a formal valuation, for a divorce, a partner buyout, an estate or an SBA loan, use a certified business appraiser.

An indicative range, backed by recast earnings and current market multiples, is enough to decide whether to sell, when, and what to fix first.
